# Facilitator answers · Scenario cards

These are the "expected" answers, and they're a starting point. The best
debriefs come from the second question: *"and why?"*

## Card 1 · The signed contract

1. Her **own private key**.
2. **Alice's public key.** Ideally in a certificate, so Bob knows it really is
   Alice's. Otherwise from a channel he already trusts.
3. Her **private key**.
4. **Yes.** Signing doesn't hide anything; it only proves origin and integrity.
   For secrecy you also need encryption.

## Card 2 · The confidential file

1. **Alice's public key.**
2. Her **public key**, preferably as a certificate.
3. Bob encrypts to Mallory's key, so Mallory can read the file (and can
   re-encrypt it to Alice so nobody notices). Bob could have noticed by
   checking the key's **fingerprint** with Alice over the phone, or by checking
   a **certificate** from a CA he trusts. This leads into Part 3: *whose padlock
   is it?*

## Card 3 · Our web shop

1. **We** (the server owner), on the server or in our key vault / HSM. The
   private key never leaves our environment.
2. A **CSR** goes to the CA; a **certificate** comes back, usually with the
   **intermediate** certificate(s).
3. **Nothing.** Public root CAs come pre-installed in browsers and operating systems.
4. The server isn't sending the **intermediate certificate**. Modern browsers
   often fetch or cache it themselves, which hides the mistake; older clients
   and many API clients don't. Fix: configure the full chain (leaf + intermediates).

## Card 4 · The internal API

1. Our **private CA's root certificate** (`ca.crt`), so they can add it to their
   trust store. Not our server certificate, and certainly not the key.
2. **Yes**, it's public information. E-mail is fine *if* they check its
   fingerprint with us through another channel (phone, the project portal).
   Otherwise an attacker could swap in their own CA certificate.
3. *"The private key stays with us; you don't need it to trust us. Here is our
   CA certificate."*
4. Anyone in the network path can pose as our API (man in the middle), and
   "just for now" tends to become permanent. It turns an authenticity check
   into security theatre.

## Card 5 · Acme calls our API (mTLS)

1. **Acme**, on its own systems.
2. **Option A:** Acme → us: **CSR**. Us → Acme: **client certificate** (plus our CA
   certificate if Acme wants it). Our server already trusts our own CA.
3. **Option B:** Acme's **CA certificate chain** (root and any intermediates),
   and which **name** (CN/SAN) their client certificate will carry.
4. The **CA certificate(s) that issue the client certificates** as trust anchor.
   Ideally also a check on the client certificate's **name or fingerprint**, so
   that no other certificate from the same CA is accepted as "Acme". This
   matters especially if the CA isn't exclusively Acme's.
5. Then **we** know Acme's private key and could pose as Acme. "Only Acme can
   call this" and non-repudiation no longer hold, and we become responsible
   for keeping their key safe. Keys are generated by whoever they identify.

**About June 2026:** under the Chrome root program, publicly trusted CAs stopped
issuing TLS certificates with the ClientAuth EKU (DigiCert by May 2026, Let's
Encrypt by May 2026, final Chrome deadline 15 June 2026). Existing certificates
work until they expire. mTLS clients need certificates from a private or
dedicated client-authentication CA.

## Card 6 · The helpful e-mail

1. The **certificate and its private key** (PKCS#12), protected by a password.
2. **Pose as our production server**: set up a look-alike, decrypt traffic from
   older setups without forward secrecy, or sign things as us. We'd have to
   treat the key as compromised from then on.
3. Probably **nothing secret at all**: to *connect to* us they only need to
   *trust* us, so our **CA certificate** (or the chain). If they must
   authenticate themselves to us, they generate *their own* key pair and we
   issue them a client certificate (card 5).
4. **Within one owner's own environment**: moving *our* certificate from our
   old server to our new load balancer, over a secure channel. The owner
   doesn't change. Sometimes a hosting provider acts *as* our server operator;
   even then, prefer that they generate a CSR on their side.

## Card 7 · Expiry Friday

1. **Acme** must renew on time. **We** only act if our configuration depends on the old certificate.
2. **Nothing**, as long as the new certificate comes from the same CA and
   carries the same name we check.
3. We must **update the pinned fingerprint** at the right moment. Too early and
   the old certificate stops working; too late and the new one doesn't work
   yet. That's why pinning individual certificates is fragile; if you pin, pin
   the CA or allow two fingerprints during rotation.
4. Agree on **renewal lead time** (e.g. 30 days before expiry), put **expiry
   monitoring** in place on both sides, and decide who gets the alert. Shorter
   lifetimes and automation (ACME protocol) make renewals routine instead of events.

## Common questions

- **"Is SSL the same as TLS?"** TLS is the successor of SSL. All SSL versions
  and TLS 1.0/1.1 are retired; use TLS 1.2 or 1.3. "SSL certificate" is just
  the old name that stuck.
- **"Is a self-signed certificate bad?"** It's a passport you stamped yourself.
  Fine if the other side installs it *knowingly* (like a private root CA); bad if
  people are trained to click "accept anyway".
- **"Isn't a password-protected .pfx safe to mail?"** The password usually
  travels by the same route, and an offline password guesser has all the time
  in the world. The real question is why the key needs to move at all.
- **"What about quantum computers?"** Browsers and OpenSSL 3.5 already mix
  post-quantum key agreement (ML-KEM) into TLS. Certificates will follow. The
  concepts from today stay the same.
